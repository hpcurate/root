## What was done

### 2 — the numpad's keys were not where they were drawn

The one that mattered most, and the one 2.22 shipped broken by being pleased
with itself.

2.22 focused the field and suppressed the keyboard with `inputmode="none"`.
That is a **hint**, and iOS honours it for drawing the keyboard and for nothing
else it does about a focused field: it still scrolls the field into view, and it
still shrinks the visual viewport in anticipation of a keyboard that never
arrives. A `position:fixed` pad drawn against a viewport that has moved under it
has its hit boxes somewhere other than its pixels — which is precisely
"registers higher up".

**The field is never focused now.** The tap that opens the pad is
`preventDefault`ed, so there is no focus, no keyboard, no scroll-into-view and
no viewport resize. Nothing moves, so nothing can be offset. The field marks
itself with a `.pad-on` class instead of relying on a caret and a focus ring it
no longer has. `inputmode="none"` is still set as a second line of defence for a
field reached by Tab.

Two supporting changes, both about the same class of unreliability:

- **The keys fire on `pointerdown`, not `click`.** A click on touch is
  synthesised, arrives late, and can be suppressed outright by anything that
  prevented a default earlier in the sequence — which 2.22's own pad did, to
  hold the focus it no longer wants. `pointerdown` is the event that actually
  names what was under the finger. The `click` handler is kept for a keyboard
  user pressing Enter on a key, de-duplicated by timestamp.
- **`touch-action:manipulation`** on the pad and its keys, so the browser is not
  holding each tap back to see whether it is the start of a double-tap zoom.

### 3 — PLAN's transition, diagnosed properly at the third attempt

2.21 made the border fade faster. 2.22 made it faster again. Neither fixed
anything, because **the border was never the problem** — and it is worth saying
what was, because two releases were spent tuning the wrong number.

`flip()` walks what is on screen *now*. Opening a project replaces the grid's
`innerHTML`, so the seven tiles you did not tap are already detached by the time
it runs: they were **never animated at all**. Most of the screen cut to its new
state on the first frame while one heading glided on for two thirds of a second.
That mismatch — everything instant, one thing slow — is what reads as a stutter,
and no fade duration can touch it.

Two fixes:

- **What leaves is animated.** `snap()` keeps the nodes as well as their rects
  (a detached node is still a node), and a new `ghostOut()` puts each one back
  as an absolutely positioned ghost at the rect it had, then fades it. They are
  gone by a third of the move, so the rows arriving read as the next thing that
  happens rather than as a second, unrelated change. A ghost has `data-flip`
  stripped from itself and every descendant — otherwise the next `snap()` finds
  two elements claiming one key — and `pointer-events:none`, so it is scenery.
  `.proj-grid` gets `position:relative` to hold them, and a note in §6 that it
  must never be given a transform.
- **The rows arrive as a wave that finishes with the move.** They started at 30%
  of the move and ran for 60% with 45ms between rows, so the fourth row was
  still arriving 110ms *after* the heading had settled — the gesture had visibly
  ended and the screen was still changing. Now 18% + 26ms apart over 42%: with
  six rows the last one lands at 610ms of a 680ms move. There is a harness check
  that does that arithmetic, so the numbers cannot drift back.

### 7 — PLAN's flag clears when you open PLAN

LOG's two rules clear themselves: writing the morning is what makes "morning not
written" stop being true, so the flag going out *is* the work being done. The
third one is different. "Nothing planned for tomorrow" is a prompt, and the
honest answer to it is often "I know — I looked, and there is nothing to plan".
A "!" that survives you checking is telling you something you have just checked.

PLAN's `onShow` now calls `LOG.dismissAlert('plan')`. The rule stays LOG's and
stays true; only the *prompt* is answered. It is the one piece of alert state
that is stored rather than derived — "I have seen this" is a fact about you and
cannot be recomputed from the record — and it is keyed by the day it was
dismissed on, so tomorrow's prompt is a new prompt. New key
**`log-alert-seen-v1`**, hyphenated for the same reason `log-scale-v2` is:
`allLogKeys()` matches `log_`.

`alertReasons()` still answers whether a rule is true; a new `alertShown()` is
what the tabs actually wear. Keeping the two separate is what lets
`dismissAlert()` ask "is this rule firing?" without asking "is it still worth
showing?". The settings preview (`alertTest`) outranks the dismissal in both
directions, so previewing the icon still works and cannot write one.

### 1 — the pinned calculator keeps its buttons

2.22 folded the ± rows and the footer away while pinned, on the theory that a
pinned counter is a readout. It is not: the buttons are the reason you pinned
it, and half a calculator stuck to the top is not a calculator. The fold is
gone; the whole widget pins.

### 4 — DAY's arrows

Back to 38×34 from two half-screen slabs, and **square** — `--r2`, the radius
every other box in the app uses — rather than the nav's pill. It is chrome, so
2.22 made it look like the nav; it is a pair of controls, so it should look like
a pair of controls, and an arrow whose edges you cannot see does not read as a
button.

**An interpretation you should check:** you scoped the revert to "in terms of
size", so I took the container back with the arrows — a compact centred stepper
rather than a full-width bar with two small arrows marooned at its ends, which
would have been a lot of chrome for two 38px controls. The idle fade you asked
for in 2.22 is untouched. If you wanted the bar itself kept full width, that is
a one-line change.

### 5 — a written day is a tint

`.lc-c.f2` was a solid `--y` fill, which made it the brightest thing on the
screen by a distance and made a month of them read as a warning rather than as a
record. Both states are washes now — 12% for a half-written day, 26% for a full
one — and the **border** carries the state, at 40% and 70% of the accent. A full
day is a step up from a half day rather than a different material.

### 6 — the fortnight

- **Stress joins energy and mood.** 2.22 argued three lines in 48px would be a
  texture rather than a reading; that was an argument for a bigger box, which is
  what you asked for. Reading the three together is most of what a fortnight is
  for — a good mood at high stress is a different fortnight from a good mood at
  low stress, and two lines could not say so.
- **The box is nearly twice as tall** (30px → 58px), which is what makes three
  legible.
- **A dot on every day that has a value**, in its series' colour. The dots are
  not decoration: a bare line says which way it went, a line of dots also says
  how often you actually answered — a fortnight with four readings and one with
  fourteen draw the same line and are not the same fortnight.
- Each dot is a **zero-length path with a round cap** and
  `vector-effect:non-scaling-stroke`. The box is stretched
  (`preserveAspectRatio="none"`) so a fortnight always fills the width, which
  would draw a `<circle>` as an ellipse; a round cap's diameter is the stroke
  width in *screen* pixels and is immune to the viewBox in both axes. Each is
  drawn twice, a surface-coloured halo under the colour, so three series
  crossing on the same day still read as three dots.
- The three hues are **fixed rather than accent-derived** (`--lc-nrg`,
  `--lc-mood`, `--lc-stress` — the same three the med slots use). Three series
  need three hues that stay apart in all fifteen themes, and an accent-relative
  palette cannot promise that. The key's swatch is the same dot the chart draws.

## Verification

`cd root/test && node harness.mjs` — **494 checks, all green, 27 new.**

- **The pad:** opening it refuses the tap that would focus the field; a tap
  opens it with the field unfocused and marked; a key pressed on `pointerdown`
  reaches the field; the click behind it does not count a second time; closing
  takes the marker off; and `auto` on a fine pointer still leaves the field
  alone entirely. Plus the source assertions: `preventDefault` before `padOpen`,
  the 700ms click de-duplication, and `touch-action:manipulation` on both the
  pad and its keys.
- **PLAN's transition:** `snap()` storing the nodes; `ghostOut()` defined and
  called from `flip()`; a ghost's `data-flip` stripped from itself *and* its
  descendants and its pointer events off; `.proj-grid` positioned and not
  transformed; the reveal timings at 18% + 26ms over 42%; and the arithmetic
  that six rows all land inside a 680ms move. Opening a project still leaves
  exactly one tile and its sections, with no ghost left claiming a flip key.
- **PLAN's flag:** flags PLAN when tomorrow is unplanned, clears on arriving at
  PLAN while the rule stays true, and is back once the dismissal's day passes.
- **The calculator:** the ± rows and footer are still in the DOM while pinned,
  and no rule hides them.
- **DAY:** the stepper compact again, `--r2` on both the bar and its arrows, and
  no `--r-pill` left in either.
- **LOG:** `f2` is a `color-mix` and not a bare `var(--y)`; three lines, three
  dot colours, dots only on days with a value, halo count matching dot count,
  the round-cap `d` attribute, the fixed hue variables, the 58px height, and a
  key naming all three series.

Also run: `node --check` over all 13 JS files and a brace-balance count over all
12 stylesheets.

**One harness check was wrong in the other direction and is fixed.** The
stepper's shape check used an unbounded lazy `[\s\S]*?` between a selector and a
declaration — which matches on into the *next* rule, so it asserted nothing
about the rule it named. Bounded with `[^}]*` now. Worth watching for elsewhere:
it is an easy way to write a check that can never fail.

**Not verified: how it looks.** The Chrome extension is still not connected.
Everything here is a fix to something you saw on a real screen, so the report is
real; the fix is verified as specified. The numpad's hit testing in particular
is a fix I am confident in on reasoning — the focus is gone, so the mechanism
that could move the pad is gone — but it is the one item here that only a real
phone can actually confirm.
